# Joint density model: structure experiments

Generated by `code/joint_model/report_x.py`, 2026-11-10. Follows `REPORT.md` (stage-1 validation). Review only: these options are in `model.py` behind flags that default to the stage-1 model, and nothing is adopted.

## Opinion

**Keep the joint model, but only with a shared drift term, and only as a check on the paper's pipeline, not a replacement for it.** With the drift term the placebo problems mostly go away and three of the four answers are stable across every choice I tested: purple urchin density falls inside reserves, lobster density rises, and sheephead density shows no detectable response. Red urchin falls by about half of what the stage-1 model said.

My recommendation on each open choice:

1. **Drift term: add it, in the regional form** (a random-walk year effect shared by all stations, plus a random-walk deviation per region). It removes the trend leak in simulation (leak 0.47 to 0.69 without it, 0.00 to 0.20 with it; false positives 23 to 60% without, 3 to 8% with) and pulls the real-data space placebo toward zero for red urchin and lobster. It costs little: no false-positive inflation at null and no measurable loss of power on the same simulated data sets. Global and regional forms do equally well in every test I ran, so the choice between them is ecological, not statistical: I do not think island and mainland reference trends are interchangeable, so I would let them differ. This choice matters for lobster (E10 +1.2 with one shared trend, +1.5 with regional trends) and red urchin (-0.8 and -0.6); the data here cannot settle it.
2. **Count model: leave it negative binomial for now.** A zero-inflated version closes only part of the gap in the predictive checks and does not move E10 for any taxon (differences of 0.00 to 0.05). Neither did capping PISCO counts (gate 7). The misfit is real, it does not reach the estimate, and it should be listed as a limitation. It will matter in stage 3, where the count distribution feeds biomass.
3. **First-increment prior: double it** (Normal(0, 1.2)). In simulation it raises the coverage of large effects (urchin layout 0.82 to 0.85 before, 0.95 after; lobster layout 0.78 before, 0.85 after) and halves their bias, with no change in the false-positive rate and intervals about 5% wider. It moves lobster from +1.53 to +1.74 and leaves the others within 0.08. Even then a lobster-sized effect is still underestimated by about 0.13, so the lobster number errs low.
4. **Regions: three** (northern Channel Islands, southern islands, mainland) instead of seven. Under seven, four regions have one to three reference stations surveyed per year, which is too few to carry a regional trend; under three, the two large regions have 17 to 29. E10 is the same under seven and three for every taxon (largest difference 0.09), so this is a choice for defensibility, not for the result.

I would also **stop presenting the `piecewise` prior as a conservative bound**. It under-covers real effects (0.62 to 0.77) and its apparent robustness to the trend leak came from shrinking everything toward zero. It is a biased estimator, not a bound. Both priors are still reported throughout this document.

What I would tell coauthors today: the joint model agrees with the direction of the paper's urchin and lobster results, says red urchin is the weakest of the three, and does not support a protection effect on sheephead density. The sheephead point needs care, because the KFM time placebo fails for sheephead under every structure: future-reserve stations were already losing sheephead relative to reference stations before 2003.
